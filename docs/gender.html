Chapter five

The gender gap, taken apart

A single gap number hides two different problems that need two different remedies. One is people being paid differently for the same job. The other is women being concentrated in jobs that pay less. For the City of New York, it is overwhelmingly the second.

How gender is inferred here, and why that matters. The City payroll does not record gender. Every figure on this page comes from matching first names against Social Security birth registrations and weighting each person by how often their name was registered male or female. That is a proxy for sex recorded at birth. It does not know anyone's gender identity, it cannot see non-binary people at all, and it misses roughly one employee in ten whose name is not in the SSA data. Treat these as estimates of a population pattern, never as facts about any individual.

Two gaps, not one

The total gap splits into a within-title part, which is what remains when you compare men and women holding the same title, and a between-title part, which is what comes from men and women being distributed differently across titles.

Why this distinction is the whole point. If the gap were mostly within-title, the remedy would be pay audits and corrections inside job classifications. Because it is mostly between-title, the remedy is about which jobs people can get into and how those jobs are valued: hiring, promotion pathways, and whether historically female-dominated titles are paid comparably to male-dominated ones doing work of similar skill. Those are slower and more political fixes, which may be why the simpler number gets quoted more often.

A second gap, on names

The same name data supports a different comparison: employees whose first name appears in US birth records against those whose does not.

Say plainly what this measures. The earlier version of this site called it "uncommon names", which sounds neutral. It is not. Whether your first name appears in American birth registrations is largely a function of whether you or your parents were born in the United States. This is a proxy for national origin and ethnicity wearing a tidy label, and it is published here because the gap it reveals is real and larger than the gender gap, not because the category is a natural one.
