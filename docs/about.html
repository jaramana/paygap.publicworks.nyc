How this works, and what it cannot see

Every number on this site can be traced back to a public dataset and a published script. This page explains where the data comes from, why a civil service title is a strange and specific thing, and the places where the method is genuinely weak.

Why this exists

The City of New York publishes its entire payroll. Names, titles, agencies, base salaries, overtime, going back to fiscal year 2014. It is one of the more remarkable acts of municipal transparency anywhere, and almost nobody uses it, because a 6.8 million row spreadsheet is not an answer to a question.

This site began with one question, asked by one person about one title: what has an Emergency Preparedness Specialist actually been paid, in real money, since 2014? Working that out by hand turned out to be genuinely useful in a room full of colleagues trying to make a case about wages. The obvious next thought was that there are about 1,500 other titles, and every one of them has people who would like the same answer.

So the test for everything here is simple. Could a civil servant open this the week before a union meeting and find the number they need? Everything that serves that goal is in. Everything that only makes the site look impressive is out.

A short history of the title

The civil service title is not a job description. It is a legal category, and understanding that explains most of what looks odd in this data.

Before the 1880s, government jobs in New York were distributed through patronage. You worked for the city because a political organisation put you there, and you could be removed the same way. The reaction against that system produced the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, signed on 16 January 1883, which established competitive examination as the basis for federal hiring. The act was drafted largely by the New York Civil Service Reform Association, written by Dorman B. Eaton and sponsored by Senator George H. Pendleton of Ohio.

New York State followed the same year, becoming the first state to adopt a merit system, on a bill sponsored in the Assembly by a young Theodore Roosevelt. The principle that survived into the present is that a public job belongs to the public rather than to whoever is in office, and that the way to make that true is to define the job in advance, test for it, and pay it on a published schedule.

That is where titles come from. A title is a defined class of work with an examination attached and a negotiated pay rate, administered today by the Department of Citywide Administrative Services. Positions fall into four jurisdictional classes: competitive, non-competitive, exempt and labor. Competitive titles are the classic case, filled from a list of people who sat an exam. DCAS runs more than 180 exams a year for over 110,000 candidates.

This is why the payroll contains close to two thousand distinct titles, many of them oddly specific, some of them holding three people, some carrying names that have not described the actual work since the 1970s. Titles accumulate. Creating one requires classification work and bargaining; retiring one strands the people in it. The result is a living archive of every job the City has ever decided it needed, which is genuinely useful for analysis and genuinely confusing for anyone trying to find themselves in it.

It also matters for pay. Because the title carries the rate, a pay argument in New York City government is usually an argument about a title: whether it is classified correctly, whether its rate has kept up, whether the work has outgrown the definition. That is the argument this site is trying to supply evidence for.

How the payroll became public

The City Council passed the Open Data Law on 29 February 2012 and Mayor Bloomberg signed it as Local Law 11 of 2012 on 7 March. It required every City agency to publish its public data sets on a single portal, with a deadline of the end of 2018, and it built on the access rights already established by the State Freedom of Information Law of 1974. Amendments in 2015, 2016 and 2017 tightened it further, including a requirement that datasets identified after the 2018 deadline still be published.

Citywide Payroll Data appeared on that portal covering fiscal year 2014 onward. It is the reason this site can exist, and it is worth saying that the law is the achievement here. The analysis is comparatively easy. Getting a municipal government to publish what it pays every one of its employees, every year, in a machine-readable format, was not.

Method

Fiscal years

New York City fiscal years run 1 July to 30 June, so FY2025 covers July 2024 through June 2025. Inflation adjustment uses the twelve-month average of the New York-area consumer price index across each fiscal year, rather than a single month's reading.

Inflation and rent

Prices are the BLS consumer price index for all urban consumers in the New York-Newark-Jersey City area, series CUURS12ASA0. Rent is the same programme's index for rent of primary residence in that area, CUURS12ASEHA. Using a national index for New York wages would understate what New Yorkers lost, which is why it is not used here. Zillow's rent index appears alongside the BLS series in dollar terms, and the difference between the two is explained on the pay against rent page.

Who is counted

Headline salary figures cover employees paid per annum with an active leave status at 30 June. Hourly and day-rate workers are analysed separately on their own page, because a rate and a salary are different quantities and averaging them together produces nonsense.

Medians, not just means

Salary distributions are right-skewed, so the mean sits above what a typical jobholder earns. Both are reported, and the median leads.

Small groups are suppressed

Any group with fewer than 30 people has its statistics withheld. A three-person agency will produce a spectacular pay gap that is really one person's salary. Suppressed rows remain in the downloadable data with a flag, so nothing is hidden, it is only kept off the charts.

Gender

The payroll does not record gender. Estimates here come from matching first names against Social Security Administration birth registrations from 1880 onward, weighting each person by the proportion of that name registered male or female. Names with fewer than 25 registrations are not used, because a handful of records would swing the weight to a certainty.

What this cannot see

  • Gender identity. The name proxy estimates sex recorded at birth. It cannot see anyone who is transgender or non-binary, and it says nothing reliable about any individual, only about large groups.
  • About one employee in ten. Name matching covers roughly 89% of the workforce, and that share falls slightly every year as the workforce diversifies. Anyone unmatched is absent from the gender figures.
  • Race and ethnicity. The payroll does not record them. The name-origin comparison on the gender page is a crude proxy for national origin and should be read as such.
  • Total public service. Tenure is time at the current agency. Someone who transferred between agencies reads as newer than they are, which makes the compression and retirement figures conservative.
  • Why anyone left. The CEASED status mixes quits, retirements, seasonal endings, layoffs and deaths with no way to separate them.
  • Whether overtime was chosen. Hours and dollars are recorded; mandated shifts and volunteered ones look identical.
  • Take-home pay. All figures are gross, before tax, pension and health contributions.
  • Whether pay is fair. This site measures what people are paid, not what the work is worth. That second question is a political one and no dataset settles it.

Corrections to the 2020 figures

An earlier version of this site ran from 2020 to 2022 on a single script that had errors in it. They changed published numbers, so anyone who cited the old site should know what moved. All six are fixed here.

  1. The pay gap formula was wrong. It divided the difference between men's and women's pay by the sum of the two rather than by men's pay. Every gap figure published was roughly half its true size. The citywide FY2021 figure shown as 1.1% should have read about 2.2%.
  2. Summary tables were computed from stale data. Each table reused a grouped data frame left over from the previous loop instead of rebuilding it, so those files did not describe the year they claimed to.
  3. The uncommon-names chart computed the gender gap instead, through a copy-paste error, and then wrote its output over the gender-gap file. There was never any published uncommon-names chart data.
  4. Inflation used the national index pinned to a single November reading, for fiscal years ending 30 June.
  5. The downloads were not machine-readable, shipping values like "$67,980" and "1.8%" as text.
  6. The 200-employee filter was applied inconsistently, to charts but not to the main download, contrary to what the site said.

Two further changes are improvements rather than fixes. Names are matched against the SSA national file instead of the New York state file, which raises coverage substantially in a city where most employees were not born in New York. And medians are reported alongside means.
