About

The Pay Gap is a lookup tool for the City of New York payroll. Search a civil service title or an agency and it reports what the job pays, how much of that comes from overtime, how long people stay, and whether the pay has kept pace with New York prices and rent since 2014.

Why

The City publishes its entire payroll: every title, agency, base salary and overtime dollar, back to fiscal year 2014. It is a remarkable piece of municipal transparency. Few people use it, because a 6.8 million row file does not answer a question.

This site joins, adjusts and totals the numbers once, so a lookup takes seconds instead of an afternoon. It is for anyone with a question about City pay: the people who do the jobs, the people who manage them, reporters, researchers, and people deciding whether to apply.

History

Title

A title is not a job description. It is a legal category, and that explains most of what looks strange in the data.

Before the 1880s, New York handed out government jobs through patronage. The reaction against that produced the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, signed 16 January 1883, which made competitive examination the basis for federal hiring. The New York Civil Service Reform Association drafted most of it, and Senator George H. Pendleton of Ohio sponsored it. New York State passed its own law the same year, the first state to adopt a merit system, on a bill Theodore Roosevelt sponsored in the Assembly.

The surviving principle is that a public job belongs to the public rather than to whoever is in office. You make that true by defining the job in advance, testing for it, and paying it on a published schedule. That is what a title is: a defined class of work with an examination attached and a negotiated rate, administered now by the Department of Citywide Administrative Services, which runs more than 180 exams a year for over 110,000 candidates.

That is also why the payroll holds well over a thousand distinct titles, many oddly specific, some holding three people, some named for work that changed decades ago. Titles accumulate: creating one takes classification work and negotiation, and retiring one strands the people in it. Because the title carries the rate, most pay questions in City government are really questions about a title. That is why this site is organized around them.

Open Data Law

The City Council passed the Open Data Law on 29 February 2012, and Mayor Bloomberg signed it as Local Law 11 of 2012 on 7 March. It required every agency to publish its data sets on a single portal by the end of 2018, on top of access rights the State Freedom of Information Law had already established in 1974. Amendments in 2015, 2016 and 2017 tightened it further.

Citywide Payroll Data appeared on that portal covering fiscal year 2014 onward. The law is the achievement here. The analysis is the easy part.

Scope

There is no name search and no individual lookup. The payroll data does list names, but the useful questions here are about titles and agencies. A way to look up a named person's salary would add little worth knowing.

Built

The site is static HTML, CSS and JavaScript, served from GitHub Pages. R does the joining, deflating and aggregating; the browser only reads the results.

The pipeline and the analysis are public in the repository, released under a BSD 3-Clause license. Every figure on this site can be rebuilt from source.

Independence

This site is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or produced by the City of New York. No agency reviewed it.

Read Method before you cite anything. It sets out how the figures are built, and, more usefully, what this data cannot tell you: gender is inferred from first names rather than recorded, tenure counts time at the current agency rather than in public service, and nothing here says whether the pay is fair.
