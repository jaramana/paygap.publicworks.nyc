About

thepaygap.nyc is a lookup tool for the City of New York payroll. Search a civil service title or an agency and it reports what the job pays, how much of that comes from overtime, how long people stay, and whether the pay has kept pace with New York prices and rent since 2014.

Why it exists

The City publishes its entire payroll: every title, agency, base salary and overtime dollar, going back to fiscal year 2014. It is a remarkable piece of municipal transparency, and it goes largely unused, because a 6.8 million row file is not an answer to a question.

This site does the joining, deflating and aggregating once so that looking something up takes a few seconds instead of an afternoon. It is built for anyone with a question about what the City pays: the people doing the jobs, the people managing them, reporters, researchers, or someone deciding whether to apply.

There is no name search and no individual lookup. The payroll does contain names, but the useful questions here are about titles and agencies, and building a way to look up a named person's salary would add little worth knowing.

A short history

The civil service title

A title is not a job description. It is a legal category, and knowing that explains most of what looks strange in the data.

Before the 1880s, government jobs in New York were handed out through patronage. The reaction against that produced the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, signed 16 January 1883, which made competitive examination the basis for federal hiring. It was drafted largely by the New York Civil Service Reform Association and sponsored by Senator George H. Pendleton of Ohio. New York State followed the same year, the first state to adopt a merit system, on a bill sponsored in the Assembly by Theodore Roosevelt.

The surviving principle is that a public job belongs to the public rather than to whoever is in office, and that you make that true by defining the job in advance, testing for it, and paying it on a published schedule. That is what a title is: a defined class of work with an examination attached and a negotiated rate, administered now by the Department of Citywide Administrative Services, which runs more than 180 exams a year for over 110,000 candidates.

It is also why the payroll holds well over a thousand distinct titles, many oddly specific, some holding three people, some named for work that changed decades ago. Titles accumulate: creating one takes classification work and negotiation, and retiring one strands the people in it. Because the title carries the rate, most pay questions in City government are really questions about a title, which is why this site is organised around them.

How the payroll became public

The City Council passed the Open Data Law on 29 February 2012 and Mayor Bloomberg signed it as Local Law 11 of 2012 on 7 March. It required every agency to publish its public data sets on a single portal by the end of 2018, building on access rights already established by the State Freedom of Information Law of 1974. Amendments in 2015, 2016 and 2017 tightened it further.

Citywide Payroll Data appeared on that portal covering fiscal year 2014 onward. The law is the achievement here. The analysis is the easy part.

The code

The pipeline and the analysis are public at github.com/jaramana/thepaygap.nyc, released under a BSD 3-Clause licence. Every figure on this site can be rebuilt from source.

Before you cite anything

Read method and limits. It sets out how the figures are built, and, more usefully, the things this data cannot tell you: gender is inferred from first names rather than recorded, tenure counts time at the current agency rather than in public service, and nothing here says whether any of the pay is fair.
